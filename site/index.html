How RAPPA works

RAPPA stands for Regularized Adjusted Predicted Points Added. It calculates how many points each team adds on an average play, adjusted for strength of schedule. The final rating turns that into points a game: how much each team is expected to beat an average team by.

RAPPA borrows its approach from basketball's RAPM (regularized adjusted plus-minus), which rates players based on who was on the floor together. Here, the "players" on each snap are one team's offense and the other team's defense.

1Predicted points

Every snap starts from a down, a distance to go and a spot on the field. From all of a season's plays, we first calculate how many points the team with the ball goes on to score from that situation before the half ends, on average, minus what its opponent scores. This is its predicted points (PP). First and goal at the 2 is worth close to a touchdown. Third and 15 from your own 5 is worth less than 0 points, because the likeliest next score belongs to the other team.

A play's value is how much it moved that number:

play value = points scored on the play + PP of the next snap (negative if the other team has the ball) − PP of this snap
  • Only runs and passes are rated here; sacks count as passes. Kicks are rated separately. Kneel-downs, spikes and penalties that wipe out a play are left out. Each play is capped at ±3 points.

2Ratings

Each team gets four main ratings: rush offense, pass offense, rush defense and pass defense. Only two are on the field for any play. RAPPA treats the play's value as the offense's rating minus the defense's:

a run by Team A against Team B: play value ≈ A's rush offense − B's rush defense ± home field

RAPPA finds the one set of ratings, for every team at once, that best predicts every play so far in a season. This is a regression, and it's where the adjustment for opponents comes from. Home field is a small bonus for the home offense on every play and the same penalty for the away offense. There are two more kinds of rating:

  • Quarterbacks get their own rating, which is included in the regression for every pass play and QB run. Each snap identifies the QB who was actually involved, so a QB's rating isn't impacted if another QB made the play.
  • Special teams also get their own rating. Punts and kickoffs are valued like plays: any points scored before the next snap, plus that snap's PP, minus what a kick from the same spot usually earns. Each team has four kicking units: punt, punt return, kickoff and kick return. Another regression splits each kick's value between the kicking and the returning unit, and the result is in points a game.

3Conferences (college only)

About two-thirds of a college team's games are against its own conference, and those don't help compare conferences. To fix this, each conference gets its own shared ratings for the same four main units, and a team's rating is its conference's rating plus its own. The whole conference's level is settled from every non-conference game its teams played.

4The starting guess

In the early weeks, each team has too few plays to trust, and a plain regression would let one lucky game make a team look elite. RAPPA uses a ridge regression instead (the "Regularized" part of the name), which pulls every rating toward a starting guess built from last season. The starting guess is different for the NFL and college, and the percentages were found by testing which values best predicted past seasons.

  • NFL: 50% of the rush offense, 30% of the pass offense, 30% of the defense, and 90% of the quarterback carry over.
  • College: each conference's average carries over in full. Then 70% of each team's gap from that average carries over for the rush offense and the defense, 55% for the pass offense, and 90% of the quarterback.

That guess is then moved for what changed over the offseason:

  • College defenses move with their recruiting, with how much of last season's defensive production comes back, and with what incoming transfers produced at their old schools.
  • College offenses move with how much of last season's rushing and receiving comes back, and with a head coaching change.
  • NFL teams move with how many of last season's snaps were played by players on their week-1 roster.

The regression pulls the ratings toward this starting guess all season, but the guess matters less and less as more plays happen.

5Final ratings and win probability

Off blends rush and pass offense (with the quarterback) at the league's mix of runs and passes. Def blends the two defenses the same way, and Net = Off + Def: how many points a play better than average the team is.

In a game, each side's edge is its offense against the other team's defense, in points per play. Each point per play is worth about 64 points a game in college and 57 in the NFL, close to the number of plays a team runs. In college the conversion also scales with how many plays the game should have: the league's recent plays per game, and how fast the two teams play. College has lost plays since the 2023 clock rules, so a fixed conversion made recent margins too big, most of all in mismatches.

predicted margin = home field + conversion × (home edge − away edge) + special teams gap + rest gap × 0.15 a day (NFL only, up to 7 days)

The final Rating is each team's predicted margin over an average team on a neutral field.

  • Win %: RAPPA's past misses on the margin fall roughly on a bell curve. A team's win chance is how much of that curve, centred on the predicted margin, lands on its side of zero. In college, the curve's width is the one that would have best called the winners of every earlier game.

6What it doesn't know

  • Injuries, except for a quarterback change, and that only once the new starter has played.
  • Weather, travel, motivation, or a coordinator getting fired midseason.
  • Anything that isn't in the play-by-play.
